Device-side top-p candidates, and an exact normalizer

livetheir P13 ↗

Astra's P13: with top-p 0.95 the host builds filtered masses by sorting 129,280 logits per row, about 16 ms a row and 50 ms of a 270 ms T=1 round. Their fix (heap-select the best 1,024) is CPU-tested only, and "partial-sort nucleus masses on device" is on their known-unknowns list.

We did the device version. One kernel per row finds the max, computes the normalizer, and ships only candidates within 10 nats of the max (cap 2,048), about 16 KB a row instead of 517 KB. Survivor counts at T=1 have a median of 14K, so shipping all survivors was never an option. The row falls back to the full logits when the candidate list can't cover it (6 of 13,032 rows).

Also check your normalizer. Our old host sampler summed 14K–50K survivors in f32, descending. Terms below about half an ulp of the running sum vanish, so z came out low (median 3.3e-5 relative, max 7.6e-4) and the top-p nucleus was smaller than exact in 4.6% of rows. This biases a lossless sampler's p/q ratio. The device z is within 6.4e-7 of f64.

verify rows 4/5/6: 119.4 / 134.7 / 149.8 → 112.3 / 125.5 / 139.1 ms (this includes merging every lane into one batched head, bit-identical; different sessions, indicative)

A live knob file for same-process A/B

livetheir drift pitfall ↗

Astra measured 7.282 tok/s before a reboot and 7.744–7.952 after on the same config, and +2.7% drift by leg order inside one boot. Their rule is to interleave legs. A reboot or restart between legs is what makes that expensive.

Our policies read /dev/shm/deepstrix-knobs.txt (NAME=value) about once a second, log every change, and publish an effective table beside it. Learned lanes, spec lanes, layer-major prefill and DSpark streams all flip live. A 10-minute-on, 10-minute-off production A/B against real traffic needs no restart, so leg order and device state stop being confounders. Their pitfall P15 (disabling a scheduler also disabled ready-first) is the same lesson: one knob per feature.

flipping lanes_learned 0→1 took effect in about 1 s; two-lane verify A/B was 6 × 10 min per arm

An online cost model for the DSpark width policy

livetheir cost table ↗

Astra's width policy runs on a per-width cost table (V[1..6] = 105.5 … 369.6 ms) fitted offline. Their notes say it went stale when top-p 0.95 became the default, refits bought only +0.6–0.8%, and the multi-session path refuses to start without a profile covering 2..N rows. One noisy step (+37%) made Algorithm 1 admit nothing until they switched to a robust affine fit.

We fit the same kind of curve online, separately per regime (one lane, two lanes, multi-stream), with epsilon-uniform exploration (1/32), fits that age out by time, and at most one sample's influence bounded. We hit the same traps twice: a single slow first two-lane sample pinned K ≤ 2 for good, and a decaying prior pinned K = 5. Exploration that does not depend on the model is what un-sticks it.

The gain is robustness, not speed: astra's own estimate for tuning is about +2%. It removes the startup profile and follows load changes.

Keep prefill from evicting decode's experts

checktheir agent turns ↗

Astra notes every small prefill at depth is an expert-streaming pass, and fixes part of it with cache-prior routing in prefill (7.1 → 4.5 s for a 150-token turn). We measured the other half: each prefill phase on our box-2 pool evicted about 2,136 experts that decode had been using, which was 22% of the next decode burst's reads. Of box-2 decode misses, 91% were experts evicted earlier (55% by speculative admits, 36% by certain reads including prefill), and 45–48% of evicted experts were needed again within 5 minutes.

What helped: a two-class LRU where prefill pages are the cheapest victims, a TinyLFU-style admission filter for speculative reads, a pinned band for the hot set, and a bulk re-read of the displaced hot experts at decode entry.

Measured on box 2's paged pool, not on a single box's GTT cache. The mechanism (prefill union overwrites decode residency) should be the same; run a trace replay on your pick log first.

HIP graphs keyed by (stage, rows)

livetheir T12 ↗

Astra lists graph capture/replay as never implemented and bounds it at 1–2% from launch gaps (~8–10 ms per DSpark round at ~5.8 µs each). Our small-row decode chain is also launch-bound, and we run graphs. What cost us time, so it need not cost them:

Key graphs by stage and row count only. Keying by layer or buffer address made ~2,240–2,560 graphs at about 2 MB of VRAM each, and a new row shape filled the dGPU in one A/B. Put the per-layer weight pointers in one device context slot written once per lane-layer, and give the kernels a twin that reads the slot. That is about 64 graphs. A stage that is a single kernel should run direct: replaying a 1-node graph costs about 6.7 µs more GPU time than launching the kernel.

Compiler traps: calling an inlined body from the direct kernel reschedules it (keep the signature, #include the body); a pointer loaded from memory is generic and turns the body into flat loads, so read the slot as an address_space(1) pointer; code after the inlined body made it reconverge (+24 s_nop). Check every direct kernel is instruction-identical to the pre-twin build by disassembly. ms.step p50 0.985× versus per-layer graphs.

Kernel techniques from the 81-idea sweep

checktheir 08 ↗

We microbenchmarked 81 ideas against the production code object at production shapes and had each win re-run by an independent reviewer. 28 were confirmed, 25 of them bit-exact against our previous kernel. The iGPU is the same gfx1151 silicon, so the techniques apply even where the code does not:

Indexer: int8-matrix scoring with query tiles held in registers (1.98×), a batched 128-wide key gather (1.35×), a hybrid sort for top-k selection (1.45×), ILP in the candidate threshold pass (1.99×). Decode attention: fused V-transpose with query in registers (2.30×), prefetching two V tiles in the weighted sum (1.29×), 128-key score blocks (1.32×), dropping a redundant f16 round trip (1.34×). Elsewhere: fused shared-expert chain (1.35×), router matvec for a 384×5120 f16 weight (2.09×), fast RMSNorm (2.57×), wave-level quantize with padded grid (1.26–2.27×), member-outer MXFP4 down kernel (1.08×).

Caveats that matter for astra: our dense path runs Q8_0 and our experts use the ggml layout, so none of this is drop-in. Their decode folds are reference-defining, so any adopted kernel must be re-pinned to their order. Kernel multipliers are medians at one shape, not end-to-end; after deploying all 28 the per-call stage timers dropped 16–44% on attention, shared expert, router, output projection and indexer.

Check C-state exit on every host wake

checktheir HSA wait ↗

C3 exit on this platform is 350 µs and a core pays it each time it wakes from a HIP stream sync. With performance governor, deep C-states off everywhere and C1 off on the cores that wait, sel_sync dropped 0.7–1.7 ms/token and the link another ~1 ms.

Astra found 76% of host CPU in HSA's busy-wait, so their waiting cores may already spin and never sleep. Then there is little to gain. One cpuidle residency read before and after a decode run tells you.

What does not transfer

Everything that depends on a second box or a discrete GPU. Also, three things astra already has in a different form.

  • dGPU/iGPU lanes, the Q8_K push, the dGPU latency kernels. They exist because attention and MoE live on different devices.
  • Box-2 paging, the exact cache mirror, fast chain, link tuning. The cost model is a network round trip; astra's is a disk read.
  • Layer-major prefill. Astra's four-lane cohorts already take one expert union per layer across 32,768 rows. Ours is a 4,096-row window.
  • Two-stream DSpark. Astra's multi-session rounds already verify several members together (1.13–1.28× aggregate). Whether their rounds batch speculative rows as aggressively as our joint K policy is not clear from the page.
  • Zero-drift staged prefetch. Same rule, same result. We adopt staged experts into a victim slot without evicting; astra's v2 measured +1.9…+3.2%.

How the two setups differ

Same model, same Strix Halo silicon, different bottleneck. Astra has one 128 GB box and about a third of the routed experts resident, so disk is the cold tier. We split the experts across two boxes' RAM, so a second machine is the cold tier and the link between them is the critical path.

Rules learned the hard way
